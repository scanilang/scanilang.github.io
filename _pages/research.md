## Working Papers
##### The Consequences of Debt and the Tradeoff of Bankruptcy
*(Job market paper)*

Bankruptcy is designed as a race-neutral financial fresh start, yet significant racial disparities persist in debt relief outcomes. Black households are more likely to file for bankruptcy. Among filers, they are more likely to file the harder and less successful Chapter 13, a 3-to-5 year repayment plan, over the quicker and highly successful Chapter 7, and less likely to complete their plans, even at the same income, assets, and debt. Even when their case is successful, Black filers leave with more debt, since they owe more nondischargeable debt, mostly student loans. Black households are also sued more for unpaid debt, which can lead to wage garnishment and drive bankruptcy filings. This paper asks: how does bankruptcy law translate racial gaps in household finances and debt collection into disparities in debt relief? Using a new dataset of detailed bankruptcy case records, I develop a structural lifecycle model with endogenous bankruptcy chapter choice, default, and repayment with plan dismissal and wage garnishment risk. The model incorporates institutional features including fee structure, repayment plan design, dischargeability rules, and wage garnishment exposure. These features interact with racial differences in household finances, including differences in liquidity, assets, debt composition, and income. Without targeting any racial moment, the model reproduces the direction of the chapter filing gap, with more Black households filing Chapter 13 even though they are more likely to be eligible for Chapter 7, because of the upfront Chapter 7 fee. Removing the upfront Chapter 7 fee requirement reverses the racial gap in chapter choice, a 12 percentage point swing that is about 40% of the gap in the data. Fewer Chapter 13 plans fail, and the share of cases that end in a discharge rises from 76% to 83%, and from 73% to 85% for Black filers. Making student loans dischargeable nearly closes the racial gap in how much debt Chapter 7 discharges, but the upfront fee still limits how many Black households can take advantage of it.

##### Racial Differences in Family Transfers: Positive vs Negative Social Tax

Racial differences in intervivos transfers between family members, including differences in timing, persistence, direction, and magnitude, have been considered a significant contributor to the racial wealth gap. Middle  and upper-income Black households are more likely than their white counterparts to have extended family members in poverty, leading to what sociologists call the "Black Tax": successful Black households assume financial responsibility for relatives who more frequently face negative shocks. In this paper, I develop a life-cycle consumption and savings model in which households face idiosyncratic shocks to both intervivos transfers and parental education transfers, and make an endogenous college enrollment decision at age 18. I use data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) and the 2013 Family Rosters and Transfers supplement to calibrate race-specific transfer functions for Black and white households, capturing how the probability and magnitude of parental education support and intervivos transfers vary with age, income, wealth, family background, and education. The model features two channels through which racial differences in family transfers can affect wealth accumulation. The first is a direct channel through differences in the flow of resources given and received over the life cycle. The second is an indirect channel through college enrollment incentives, which are shaped both by differences in parental education transfers and by the expectation of future transfer obligations. To assess the importance of these disparities, I simulate a counterfactual in which Black households receive the same parental education transfers as white households.
